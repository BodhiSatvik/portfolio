Workshop 1 · AIML-500

AI & ML History Timeline

Objective
To research and visualize key milestones in AI history from 1950 to the present, tracing the field's founding, its funding collapse during the First AI Winter, its recovery through expert systems, and its move into mainstream use through generative AI.
Process
I researched primary and credible secondary sources, including Our World in Data's history of AI, an IEEE Computer Society overview of AI's evolution, and the assigned course documentary on AI's history, to build a chronological account covering ten major milestones from Turing's 1950 paper through the generative AI boom of 2020–2022. From that research, I used Canva to generate a visual timeline, then selected and refined five of the milestones (Turing's Question, the Dartmouth Conference, the First AI Winter, the Expert Systems Boom, and the Generative AI Mainstream era) into the final infographic shown here.
Tools and Technologies Used
Canva Gemini (research assistance) Our World in Data IEEE Computer Society
Value Proposition (Artifact-Specific)
This timeline gives technical and non-technical audiences alike a fast, visually grounded way to see that AI's history isn't a straight line: it includes real funding collapses driven by overpromising, not just steady progress.
Unique Value
Rather than treating AI history as a simple success story, this timeline puts the First AI Winter, a period where AI funding was cut after the field failed to deliver on its own hype, alongside the eventual recovery through expert systems and today's generative AI boom. That contrast is a useful reference point for evaluating whether current AI momentum reflects durable progress or another hype cycle.
Relevance
As someone who evaluates and integrates AI/ML tools professionally, understanding this history directly informs how I assess new AI capabilities and communicate their limits to stakeholders who weren't around for the earlier boom-and-bust cycles.

Workshop 2 · AIML-500

Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning: A Comparative Report

Objective
To analyze two real-world AI systems, one built on traditional machine learning and one built on deep learning, and explain why each system's underlying approach fits its problem, while the other approach would not.
Process
I selected two production systems with well-documented architectures: Gmail's spam filter, which relies on logistic regression and hand-defined features, and Facebook's DeepFace facial recognition system, which relies on a nine-layer convolutional neural network trained on millions of images. For each, I researched how the system actually works, then argued from the structure of the problem itself (are the important features nameable by a human, or not) to explain why its approach fits and the alternative approach would not.
Tools and Technologies Used
Logistic Regression Convolutional Neural Networks Claude (research assistance)
Value Proposition (Artifact-Specific)
This report shows I can look at a real production system and reason about why its designers picked the model family they did, not just recite the textbook definitions of ML and DL.
Unique Value
A lot of ML/DL comparisons stop at "deep learning is more powerful." This report argues the opposite case too: that picking the simpler, more interpretable model is the right engineering call whenever the problem's features are already known and nameable, and that reaching for a neural network by default is often solving a harder problem than the one in front of you.
Relevance
This is the exact judgment call I make professionally when evaluating whether a proposed ML solution actually fits a production problem, or whether it's reaching for more model complexity than the problem requires.

Workshop 3 · AIML-500

Explaining Neural Networks in Plain English

Objective
To take a technical machine learning concept, how a neural network works, and explain it so a non-technical reader can actually follow it, using deliberate teaching techniques rather than a textbook definition.
Process
I started from the core ideas I wanted a reader to walk away with (weights, layers, learning by correction, and activation functions), then built the explanation from a familiar scene, a toddler being corrected until she can tell a cat from a dog, before naming a single technical term. Each paragraph introduces exactly one new idea and builds on the one before it, so the concept assembles itself instead of arriving all at once. I worked with a generative AI tool to iterate on the wording, cutting jargon and tightening the build-up over several passes.
Tools and Technologies Used
Claude (drafting & iteration) Communication-for-learning strategies Chunking & progressive disclosure
Value Proposition (Artifact-Specific)
This piece shows I can translate a technical idea for whatever audience is in front of me, which is the difference between knowing something and being able to lead others through it.
Unique Value
My first two artifacts show research and technical analysis. This one shows the communication half: taking something I understand and making it land for someone who doesn't share my background. It uses named techniques from the workshop, leading with a concrete example, introducing one idea at a time, defining each term the moment it appears, rather than just "writing it more simply."
Relevance
Most of my real influence as a senior engineer comes from explaining technical tradeoffs to people who don't live in the code, product managers, leadership, engineers on other teams. An artifact that proves I can do that clearly is as job-relevant as any system I've built.
